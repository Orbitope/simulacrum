Simulacrum · vectorizing RL environments

Make your environment 100× faster. Keep the one you can read.

Teaching an agent anything means running your simulator a few million times. Written the normal way, one step at a time in Python, that's the slowest thing you own. Rewrite it so ten thousand copies step at once and it goes about a hundred times faster. It also stops being code you can read, and it might quietly not be the same game. Here's how to take the speed and keep the version you understand. Everything on this page is running for real in your browser.

First, the thing itself

This is forager. It's the whole game.

An 8×8 grid. Six berries worth 0.10, 0.15 and 0.30 energy. You start with 1.0 and burn 0.02 a step, so you've got fifty steps unless you eat. Pick a direction, move one square, eat what you land on.

One wrinkle: 15% of the time a gust spins your move 90° clockwise. You meant to go north, you went east. That's the only randomness in here, and it's the thing that makes everything later hard.

Press Step a few times. It's playing itself with random moves.

That's it. That's the environment. Now hold onto something: to teach an agent to play even this, you need millions of steps. Not thousands. And you don't want one copy of that board, you want ten thousand of them running side by side, because that's the only way you keep a GPU busy.

So the question stops being "does my simulator work" and becomes "how fast can I run ten thousand of them".

Why bother

Your simulator is the bottleneck. Not your GPU.

Here's the shape of it. Your agent learns by playing: try something, see what happened, nudge the weights, repeat, a few million times over. The learning half runs on a GPU and is genuinely quick. The playing half runs in your simulator, one step at a time, in Python. Guess which one you spend the afternoon waiting on.

Rewriting the simulator so every copy advances in a single tensor operation is what removes the ceiling. Here's what that's actually worth, measured on the two example environments in the repo:

Two things there are worth stopping on. The first is the size of it: ninety-two times the readable implementation, and still climbing at N = 8192.

The second is more useful to you. Look at N = 1. The batched version is slower than the plain Python one, 13,567 steps/s against 385,199. Vectorizing isn't free speed you sprinkle on top. It's a trade that doesn't pay until you're running a few hundred copies at once. If you only ever run one environment at a time, you already have the fast one.

The catch

The fast one is a different program.

The readable version has one agent and an if. The fast version has ten thousand agents and no branches at all, because you can't branch per-instance across a batch. Every if turns into arithmetic that works out both answers and throws one away.

Now, the obvious move is to write the fast one, check it looks right, and delete the slow one. Everybody does this. It feels safe, because you tested it. You tested it against the fast one.

And here's the part that should worry you: a subtly wrong simulator still gives you a training curve that goes down. Your agent gets better at something. You just don't find out it was the wrong something until much later, if ever.

The proposal

Don't delete the slow one. Make it earn its keep.

Write the environment twice. Once so a person can read it: explicit ifs, one instance, every line traceable back to a rule. Once so ten thousand copies can run it. Both get written from a single spec.md, and never from each other.

Then let a machine check, step by step, that the two produce bit-identical states, rewards, terminations and observations. If they agree, either you got the spec right twice, or you made the same misreading twice in two completely different programming styles. The second one is a much weaker coincidence than getting it right once by luck.

What you get out of that is specific. The readable version stays the thing you debug against, reason about and explain your results with. The fast one is provably the same environment. You're not trading interpretability for throughput, you're keeping the first and adding the second.

Three things have to work for that to hold up. The rest of this page is those three, each one running live.

What you're installing

Simulacrum is the part you'd otherwise write yourself.

None of this is a technique you hand-roll per project. It's a Python package. pip install, then simulacrum new myenv drops the skeleton, and you fill in four files.

Underneath, the package hands you the awkward machinery you'd otherwise get subtly wrong: a counter-based RNG in both a scalar and a tensor flavour, guaranteed to agree bit-for-bit; a BatchedEnv base class that owns the step loop, in-tensor auto-reset, terminal-state capture and invariant checking; trajectory read/write against your schema; and the report gate. forager's fast.py is about sixty lines, because the base class handles everything that isn't the game.

Requirement one

Two programs can't agree unless they roll the same dice.

This is the problem that kills most attempts before they start. The readable version draws its randomness one instance at a time. The batched version draws for ten thousand at once and bins most of them. With a normal random generator, the kind that keeps a position in a stream, those binned draws still advance the stream, and your two programs come apart permanently on step one.

So simulacrum throws the stream away. Every random decision is a pure function of four numbers:

bits = hash(episode_key, step, slot, index)

No history, no cursor, nothing to keep in sync. A slot names which decision this is. index separates repeats of the same decision, one per berry, say. Drag the sliders. The draw depends on those four numbers and nothing else.

Now hit "draw them out of order". Nothing moves.

That's the whole trick, and three hard problems fall out of it at once. The batched code can draw for all ten thousand instances and throw most away, because a binned draw was never part of a sequence. An episode that ends early can re-key and restart in place without telling anybody. And porting to another array library is mechanical, because there's no hidden state to port.

That last one isn't hypothetical. The grid you played with up top is running on a 90-line JavaScript port of this exact generator.

Requirement two

Break it on purpose and watch the test catch it.

Two copies, same seed, same actions. Readable on the left, batched on the right. One bug is already switched on, so you land on a real divergence. Untick it and watch 300 steps come back identical, then go break something else.

Each one shows you the actual line of code that causes it, and what it costs you at training time. These aren't invented. They're the mistakes people make translating a readable environment into tensors, and most of them leave a perfectly trainable environment behind.

Why you should care

What a broken environment actually costs you.

"The states diverge at step six" is easy to shrug at. So here's the same thing in money terms: take a berry-seeking policy, score it in the broken environment the way your training dashboard would, then run that same policy in the real one and see what you actually get.

These are live numbers, computed in your browser over 50 episodes as you click. And they don't all fail the same way:

Three different disasters, and only one of them looks like a disaster while it's happening.

The berry bug is the loud one: your dashboard reads +11.5 and you ship, and the thing scores −3.3 in the real world, because it learned a map that doesn't exist. The reward bug is the quiet one that costs you a month: your dashboard reads −2.3 for a policy that's actually worth +9.2, so you conclude your algorithm is broken and go tune hyperparameters that were never the problem. And the float bugs change no behaviour at all, they just mean nobody, including you, can ever reproduce your run.

Honest about what this is: a hand-written policy scored in both environments, not a trained agent. A real training run would additionally adapt to the broken dynamics, which generally widens these gaps rather than closing them.

Requirement three

And now the bug that only exists in a crowd.

Everything so far compares the batched version against the readable one, and the readable one only knows how to be a single instance. So that comparison runs at N = 1, and the entire reason you're here is to run at N = 8192. There's a hole in that, and here's what falls through it.

Somewhere in your batched code, a mask shaped [N] meets something shaped [N, 1]. In NumPy and PyTorch that isn't an error. It's a broadcast, and it quietly hands you an [N, N] matrix that a reduction downstream flattens back into a perfectly reasonable-looking [N]. One keyword:

At one instance, that accidental matrix is 1×1, which is exactly the right answer. So the bug isn't just missed, it's mathematically invisible at the only batch size a reference comparison can ever run at. And it shows up the moment you scale, which is to say the moment you started caring.

Which is why the guarantee needs a second leg with no reference implementation in it at all. Run instance i alone. Run it again inside a batch of eight. Demand the two be bit-identical. Drag N up and watch the rows go red.

Making it stick

Nobody remembers to run the tests.

So it isn't left up to you. The ten tests write a report, and your training script reads it and refuses to start if it's missing, failing, or older than your source files. Tweak fast.py, go straight back to training, and you get stopped, because the report no longer describes the code you're about to run.

Here's the real thing, from simulacrum validate examples/forager. Same environment you were poking at up top.

Evidence, not proof. Two implementations agreeing means the same misreading would have had to happen twice, in two very different styles. That is a weak coincidence, not a formal verification.
The page's JavaScript shares an author with the Python. It is a third implementation from the same spec, checked bit-for-bit across 1,500 steps, not an independent audit. What it shows is that the spec is precise enough to rebuild from.
What it costs

You write the environment twice. That's the bill.

Worth saying plainly, because it's a real cost: a second implementation, plus the discipline to write it from the spec instead of from the first one. What you get back is that the readable version stops being a liability you'll eventually delete, and becomes the thing that certifies the fast one on every single run.

And if you already have an environment, you're most of the way there. The thing you've got is the reference. Write down its spec, add a batched one, and let the battery tell you where they disagree. Every divergence it finds is a place your spec was ambiguous, which you wanted to know anyway.

Keep the one you can read.

Going fast shouldn't cost you the ability to explain what your simulator does. Write it once for people, once for the hardware, and let a machine hold both to the same spec. Then the speed comes with a receipt.
